Setup Tips

RC Ride Height and Droop Setup Guide

Common Symptoms

  • Unsure how to balance front and rear ride height on a setup gauge
  • Chassis bounces or skitters over track seams, curbs, or bumps
  • Looking to understand how droop (extension travel) controls weight transfer

5 Steps to Try First

  1. Start with level ride height or set rear 0.5mm higher … A slight nose-down rake transfers load onto the rear tires under acceleration, adding traction.
  2. Measure in ready-to-run condition with battery installed … Electronics and battery weight compress the suspension, making unweighted measurements inaccurate.
  3. Avoid setting ride height excessively low … When the chassis strikes the track or curbs, the tires unload instantly, frequently triggering sudden spins or rolls.
  4. Add more droop on low-grip or bumpy surfaces … Allowing the suspension arms to extend further keeps the inside tires in contact with the ground through corners.
  5. Reduce front droop to sharpen directional transitions … Restricting front upward travel limits excessive weight transfer and crisps up steering response.

※ These are general guidelines.
Setup effects can vary depending on chassis and surface grip. It's best to change one setting at a time.

A Closer Look at Ride Height and Droop

Ride Height vs. Droop: Key Differences

SettingDefinitionPrimary Influence on Handling
Ride HeightClearance from flat ground to chassis undersideControls center of gravity; front-to-rear rake alters weight transfer dynamics.
Droop (Downstop)Down travel of suspension beyond ride heightControls bump absorption, inside-wheel contact in roll, and chassis agility.

Front-to-Rear Ride Height Balance (Rake)

Ride height serves as the baseline foundation for all other suspension adjustments.
Beginning with equal clearance front and rear is standard practice, but slightly raising the rear by roughly 0.5mm is a proven trackside tuning technique.
This forward rake encourages positive forward bite onto the rear tires during corner exit, calming nervous handling.

Suspension Droop and Mechanical Grip

Droop represents how far the suspension arms can drop when the chassis lifts in roll or pitch.
On dusty outdoor asphalt or bumpy tracks, generous rear droop allows inside tires to remain pressed against the surface through turns, preventing snap oversteer.
Conversely, on high-grip indoor carpet where the car feels sluggish in chicanes, limiting front droop keeps the chassis flatter and sharpens steering transitions.

How to Measure Accurately

Turning the shock spring pre-load collars changes both ride height and droop simultaneously.
To tune systematically, first set maximum arm down-travel evenly using downstop screws on the suspension arms, and then adjust spring pre-load collars to achieve your target ride height.

When One Change Alters Another: Suspension Interdependence

Because suspension links form an interconnected geometry, altering a single setting frequently causes other alignment angles to shift.
For instance, raising or lowering ride height rotates the suspension arms, which directly alters both camber angles and toe settings.
Likewise, changing shock mounting holes or running worn tires with smaller outer diameters will naturally shift your ride height and camber.
To avoid chasing setup gremlins, following a systematic tuning sequence is recommended:
1. Set suspension droop (downstop travel) → 2. Adjust ride height with spring collars → 3. Re-check and set camber angles → 4. Verify toe angles and steering trim.
Getting into the habit of re-checking camber whenever you adjust ride height helps prevent mysterious handling inconsistencies.

Avoid Going Too Far

Slamming the chassis onto the ground for aggressive looks causes chassis slap over bumps, destroying traction.
Similarly, removing droop entirely makes the chassis skitter over curbs because the wheels cannot extend into dips.
Ensure the suspension has adequate breathing room in both compression and extension to absorb track irregularities smoothly.
